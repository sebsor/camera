Aperture is the opening inside the lens that light passes through. It's measured in f-stops — confusingly, a smaller number means a bigger opening.

A wide opening (small f-number, like f/1.4) lets in lots of light and throws the background out of focus — that creamy blur is called bokeh. A narrow opening (large f-number, like f/16) lets in less light but keeps more of the scene sharp front-to-back.

Try it: portraits usually want a wide aperture (low f-number) so the background melts away. Landscapes usually want a narrow aperture (high f-number) so everything stays sharp.

Shutter speed

1/125s

Freezes most everyday motion

1/2000 — FAST1s — SLOW

Shutter speed is how long the sensor is exposed to light for a single shot. It's measured in fractions of a second.

A fast shutter (1/1000s) freezes motion sharp — good for sports or kids running around. A slow shutter (1/4s or slower) blurs anything moving during the exposure — that's how you get silky waterfalls, or light trails from cars at night. It also lets in more light, which matters once it gets dark.

Watch the dot in the box above: at fast speeds it's frozen in place. Slow it down and it smears into a streak — that streak is motion blur.

ISO

ISO 400

Good general-purpose sensitivity

ISO 100 — CLEANISO 12800 — GRAINY

ISO is how sensitive the sensor is to the light it receives. Low ISO (100) gives the cleanest image but needs plenty of light. Raising the ISO brightens a dark scene, but the trade-off is noise — that grainy, speckled texture you can see building up above.

Aperture, shutter speed, and ISO together make up the "exposure triangle." Open the aperture, slow the shutter, or raise the ISO — each one brightens the shot, but each has its own side effect (less depth of field, more motion blur, or more noise).

Rule of thumb: keep ISO as low as the light allows, and only raise it once aperture and shutter speed are already doing what you need creatively.
