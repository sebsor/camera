f/1.4 — WIDE OPENf/22 — STOPPED DOWN

Aperture is the opening inside the lens that light passes through. It's measured in f-stops — confusingly, a smaller number means a bigger opening.

A wide opening (small f-number, like f/1.4) lets in lots of light and throws the background out of focus — that creamy blur is called bokeh. A narrow opening (large f-number, like f/16) lets in less light but keeps more of the scene sharp front-to-back.

Try it: portraits usually want a wide aperture (low f-number) so the background melts away. Landscapes usually want a narrow aperture (high f-number) so everything stays sharp.

Shutter speed

1/125s

Freezes most everyday motion

1/2000 — FAST1s — SLOW

Shutter speed is how long the sensor is exposed to light for a single shot. It's measured in fractions of a second.

A fast shutter (1/1000s) freezes motion sharp — good for sports or kids running around. A slow shutter (1/4s or slower) blurs anything moving during the exposure — that's how you get silky waterfalls, or light trails from cars at night. It also lets in more light, which matters once it gets dark.

Watch the dot in the box above: at fast speeds it's frozen in place. Slow it down and it smears into a streak — that streak is motion blur.

ISO

ISO 400

Good general-purpose sensitivity

ISO 100 — CLEANISO 12800 — GRAINY

ISO is how sensitive the sensor is to the light it receives. Low ISO (100) gives the cleanest image but needs plenty of light. Raising the ISO brightens a dark scene, but the trade-off is noise — that grainy, speckled texture you can see building up above.

Aperture, shutter speed, and ISO together make up the "exposure triangle." Open the aperture, slow the shutter, or raise the ISO — each one brightens the shot, but each has its own side effect (less depth of field, more motion blur, or more noise).

Rule of thumb: keep ISO as low as the light allows, and only raise it once aperture and shutter speed are already doing what you need creatively.

Lenses

A lens's name has two parts. Yours is the RF-S 18-45mm F4.5-6.3 IS STM — the 18-45mm is the focal length range (more on the F4.5-6.3 part below). Focal length controls how much of the scene fits in frame: small numbers are wide-angle and fit a lot in, big numbers are telephoto and reach far into a scene, like binoculars. Watch the little figure above: same scene, same distance, just a different focal length zooming the frame in around it.

The F4.5-6.3 part works exactly like the f-stop dial from the Aperture tab — it's telling you the widest this lens can open. Yours is a zoom lens, so that widest opening actually changes as you zoom: F4.5 at 18mm, narrowing to F6.3 by 45mm. A prime lens — one fixed focal length, like the classic RF 50mm F1.8 — skips that trade-off: one fixed, usually wider opening for less money, because it doesn't need the extra glass a zoom does.

Your R10 has an APS-C sensor with a 1.6× crop factor, so your 18-45mm actually frames more like a 29-72mm would on a full-frame camera (18 × 1.6 ≈ 29, 45 × 1.6 = 72) — worth knowing so a shot doesn't look tighter or wider than you expected compared to photos you've seen from full-frame cameras.

Custom shooting modes (C1/C2)

The R10's mode dial has two custom slots. Each one saves a full snapshot — shooting mode, aperture/shutter, AF behavior, white balance, everything — so turning the dial to C1 or C2 instantly recalls a whole recipe instead of you resetting five menus by hand. Two settings suggestions below, built around your kit lens's actual limits rather than an ideal you don't have the glass for yet.

C2 — Nature

  • Shooting modeAV (APERTURE-PRIORITY)
  • ApertureF8–F11

    The opposite instinct from portraits — a narrower aperture keeps a landscape sharp from foreground to background instead of isolating one subject.

  • Subject to detectNO PRIORITY

    Switch this to Animals instead if "nature" means wildlife for you more than landscapes — worth having two variants in mind even though there's only one C2 slot to actually store.

    A crooked horizon is one of the most common landscape mistakes — the level makes it hard to miss.

  • ISOAUTO, 100–3200

    Capped lower than the portrait preset — landscapes are usually shot more deliberately, so trading a slower shutter for a cleaner file is normally the right call.

Saving a preset

  1. Dial in every setting above by hand first — mode, aperture, AF, white balance, ISO.
  2. Tools menu (wrench icon) → Custom shooting mode (C1, C2) → Register settings.
  3. Choose C1 or C2 and confirm — that snapshot is now saved to the dial position.
  4. Turn the mode dial to C1 or C2 any time to recall it instantly.

One deliberate deviation from the R10 setup guide elsewhere on this tab: it suggests leaving Auto update enabled, which lets a custom mode silently absorb whatever you change mid-shoot. That's convenient once you know exactly what you're doing. For two intentional recipes you're building right now, I'd leave it off instead — otherwise an in-the-moment tweak quietly overwrites the "Portraits" preset you were trying to build, and it stops being a reliable recipe.

General beginner habits that matter more than gear — composition, light, the mistakes almost everyone makes early on, and how to practice in a way that actually improves your eye.

Synthesized from a range of photography educators and composition guides — these are starting habits worth knowing, not hard rules.

Try it — rule of thirds

The figure is already placed near the upper-right intersection point rather than dead center — that's the rule of thirds at work, whether or not the grid is visible. Your R10 can show this same grid live in the viewfinder if you turn it on in the shooting menu.

Composition

  • Use the rule of thirds as a default, not a law

    Split the frame into a 3×3 grid and place your subject along a line or at an intersection instead of dead center. It's a starting habit that consistently produces balanced shots — not a rule that always applies.

  • Look for leading lines

    Paths, fences, rivers, the edge of a building — anything that draws the eye toward your subject. Point them into the frame, flowing toward what you want noticed.

  • Fill the frame

    The single most common beginner habit is standing too far back. Get physically closer, or use more focal length, until your subject actually dominates the shot.

  • Leave room to breathe

    Empty space around a subject (negative space) isn't wasted — it gives the eye somewhere to rest and makes the subject read more clearly.

  • Know when to break it

    Strong symmetry, repeating patterns, or a deliberately centered portrait can all beat a rule-of-thirds composition. Learn the habit first, then learn when to ignore it.

Light

  • Golden hour is the forgiving window

    The hour after sunrise and the hour before sunset give warm, low-angle, soft light — the most flattering light of the day, and hard to get badly wrong.

  • Midday sun is the hard mode

    Straight-overhead sun causes squinting and harsh shadows under eyes and noses. If you're stuck shooting at noon, move your subject into open shade or put the sun behind them instead of in front.

  • Overcast days are a secret weapon

    Cloud cover works like a giant natural softbox — genuinely one of the best conditions for portraits, even though it looks unremarkable to the eye.

  • Direction matters as much as amount

    Side light reveals texture and shape, front light flattens it, and backlight can create a glowing edge or a silhouette depending on how you expose for it.

Common mistakes

  • Centering everything by default

    Subject, horizon, all dead center. Fine sometimes, but worth questioning as a first instinct rather than a habit.

  • Camera shake at slow shutter speeds

    A rough rule of thumb: keep your shutter speed close to 1/(your focal length in mm) unless you're on a tripod. Your kit lens's image stabilization buys you some slack below that.

  • Forgetting settings from your last session

    Showing up to shoot without checking that the ISO or exposure compensation isn't still wherever you left it last time. Worth a glance before your first shot.

  • Chasing gear instead of practice

    A better lens won't fix composition or timing — those come from shooting a lot and reviewing honestly, not from buying more equipment.

  • Only ever shooting from eye level

    Crouch down, get up high, change your angle. The same subject from a different height is often a far more interesting photo.

Practice habits

  • Shoot often, review honestly

    The fastest way to improve is taking a lot of photos and actually looking back at what worked and what didn't — not just taking more of them.

  • Try a one-lens or one-subject constraint

    Limiting your choices for a session forces you to solve framing problems creatively instead of relying on zooming or switching gear.

  • Back up your photos properly

    The classic rule is 3 copies, on 2 different types of storage, with 1 kept somewhere else entirely. Losing a card or a drive is a common, completely avoidable heartbreak.

  • Learn from people a few steps ahead, not just fellow beginners

    Swapping tips with people at your exact stage caps how fast you improve. Look for photographers with more experience, not just more enthusiasm.
